Fleet vNext

Today you look at Fleet through sessions: machine, then folder, then session. That works with one or two machines. With four machines and a dozen agents running, the list grows faster than you can read it, and nothing tells you which session is the one you should look at. And one machine can't do all the work anyway.

The idea here: work becomes the unit, machines become capacity, and you become the scarce part. You, or an orchestrator agent, post pieces of work. Every machine you own offers seats, and a free seat picks up work that fits. You see work and decisions, not sessions. Sessions are still there, one click in.

UnitA piece of workan ask, a folder, what it needs, what done means
CapacitySeats on machinesidle seats pull work; nothing sits unused
BottleneckYour attentiona seat waiting on you is the most expensive seat

Three concepts below show the same afternoon three ways: A Queue (closest to today), B Lanes (machines over time), C Brief (the orchestrator as your home screen). They aren't exclusive; vNext could be C as home, with A and B one click away.

New ideas

Things a fleet of your own machines can do

Context affinity

Work goes where its context is warm

A machine that just worked on ledger-api has it checked out, its build cache full, its prompt cache warm and its notes fresh. The matcher prefers it for follow-ups, and the card says why: "atlas: has ledger-api, built 20 min ago".

Idle chores

Spare seats do the dull work

A backlog you approve in advance: flaky tests, dependency bumps, TODOs marked for agents. A seat idle for 10 minutes takes one. Results wait as draft PRs. Chores never merge and never interrupt you.

Lend a seat

Your laptop works while you're away

When you lock the screen, your own machine offers a seat or two; when you come back it finishes the step it's on and takes nothing new. The machine you type on is never slowed down by agents.

You are the bottleneck

Waiting on you, measured

Seat time blocked on a question or a review is counted and shown. Decisions are sorted by how much work they unblock, not by when they were asked. The phone app shows only this list.

Checkpoints

A machine can drop out mid-piece

Each piece pushes its work branch at every finished todo. If its machine stops answering, the piece resumes on another machine from the last push with the session's notes, as attempt 2.

Seats have a price

Roles map per machine

Fast on build-box can be a local model at $0; Strong only runs where an Opus sign-in exists. The matcher reads price as well as fit, and every goal shows what it has cost so far.

In the wild

What exists, and where the gap is

Coding-agent tools all treat machines as either one laptop or a vendor's cloud. Systems that do spread work over many machines (CI runners, task queues) know nothing about agents, folders or people. Fleet already sits on your own machines and talks to every harness, so it can join the two.

How a machine picks up work

Pull, without the machines calling home

01 Posted

A piece of work: the ask, the folder, a role, labels it needs, what "done" means, and what it waits for.

02 Matched

When a seat frees up, Fleet finds the oldest piece that fits: labels, folder, harness. Pinned work goes only to its machine.

03 Claimed

Fleet starts a session on that machine, in a worktree, with the hand-off it already has. The claim holds while the machine answers.

04 Runs

A normal session. Questions go to Needs you, or to the orchestrator that posted it.

05 Back

Done when its check passes, e.g. a PR with green checks. If the machine stops answering, the piece goes back to the queue as attempt 2.

Who does the matching

Today the interface Fleet reaches out to nodes; nodes never know where the interface is. Keeping it that way is simpler and safer: the Fleet with the queue (your main one) already watches every machine live, so it sees a free seat and hands the next piece over through the same hand-off agents use now. It behaves like pull, a free seat takes work, but nothing new has to reach in from outside. True pull, where a node asks a queue for work, only matters once there are nodes the main Fleet can't reach, and that can come later.

Decisions for you

Four questions before anything is built

1. Who decides where work runs?

  1. Free seats take the next piece that fits; you or the orchestrator can pin a piece to a machine recommended
  2. The orchestrator always picks the machine
  3. You always pick, Fleet only queues

2. What is the orchestrator?

  1. A normal session that plans and posts; Fleet does the handing out recommended
  2. A standing agent that runs all the time and dispatches every piece itself (tokens on every hand-out)
  3. No orchestrator agent; you post work yourself

3. Which concept leads?

  1. A Queue first, replacing Board; B Lanes as its second tab; C Brief once the orchestrator exists recommended
  2. C Brief as Fleet's home screen from the start
  3. Something else from these three, or none of them

A is the cheapest to build and teaches the model. C is the most different, but it needs an orchestrator that is good at planning before it's worth opening.

4. What is "an agent" on the Agents tab?

  1. A seat on a machine: it runs one piece at a time with the role the piece asks for recommended
  2. Named, standing agents ("Reviewer on build-box") that only take their kind of work

Named agents feel closer to what you described, but they split seats into pools that sit idle. Roles on the piece get the same effect.

Seats and labels per machine, and the matcher across machines. Lost machine puts the piece back as attempt 2.
